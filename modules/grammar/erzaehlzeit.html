Narrating the past: Präteritum vs. PlusquamperfektDie Erzählzeit im Präteritum und Plusquamperfekt

1 · The concept

German narrates in two layers. One tense carries the story, and that tense is normally the Präteritum. The second tense marks what had already happened before that story reached the point it is now at, and that is the Plusquamperfekt. Getting the two apart is less a question of dates than of the narrator’s point of view: which event is the line the reader is standing on, and which event lies behind it.

1. The Präteritum carries the narrative

When German writes about the past – in reports, memoirs, news features, academic accounts, novels – the Präteritum is the default. The learner’s instinct is often to reach for the Perfekt, which is the tense of spoken German. That is not wrong, but a page of Perfekt reads as informal, and in B2 writing it is worth shifting down into the past.

The Präteritum is used for all finite persons of a verb, which is why its forms look so economical. sein is the exception: it takes war in every person, with no person ending at all.

The Präteritum has no person endings. ich ging, ich sah, ich kam – one form covers every person, so ich ging can never acquire an -te- or any other ending. Weak verbs simply add -te- to the stem, which is why the forms look so regular.

2. The Plusquamperfekt marks the earlier event

The Plusquamperfekt is formed with hatte or war plus the participle II: Ich hatte den Brief schon gelesen. Formally it is identical to the Perfekt. The only thing that separates the two is the reference point: in a narrative, hatte gelesen is read as read before the moment now being described, and the same form in a conversation is read as read at some point up to now.

SituationReadingExample
Present relevance (Perfekt)at some time up to nowIch habe den Film dreimal gesehen.
Earlier than a past event (Plusquamperfekt)before the event now narratedIch hatte den Film dreimal gesehen, bevor wir ins Kino gingen.

Adverbs make the earlier layer explicit and are the learner’s safest guide: schon, bereits, vorher, zuvor, früher, jahrelang, damals. If you can insert vorher without changing the meaning, the Plusquamperfekt is right.

hatte or war?

Verbs of movement and change of state take war: war gegangen, war gekommen, war gefahren, war geblieben, war geworden. Verbs of activity take hatte: hatte gearbeitet, hatte gewohnt, hatte gekannt, hatte bekommen. With a direct object, haben is normally forced: Ich hatte ihn nie gesehen, not Ich war ihn nie gesehen. For verbs that allow both, the states of experience and lasting result prefer war: Ich war noch nie dort gewesen.

Verb-final in the subordinate clause. Nachdem ich gegessen hatte – not Nachdem ich hatte gegessen. The same holds after als, wenn, weil, bevor, sobald and wie.

3. The Plusquamperfekt with modal verbs

A modal verb in the second past drops its infinitive entirely, and the participle is irregular, with no ge-:

ModalPartizip IIPlusquamperfekt
könnengekonntEr hatte nicht kommen können.
müssengemusstWir hatten früher um sechs aufstehen müssen.
dürfengedurftSie hatte das Foto nicht zeigen dürfen.
sollengesolltDie Firma hatte den Kunden früher informieren sollen.
wollengewolltNiemand hatte die Diskussion abbrechen wollen.

A second, lexical verb is kept in the infinitive, and the two infinitives close the clause: Ich hatte zwei Stunden warten müssen. A separable prefix stays whole inside that final position: Wir hatten den Termin absagen müssen.

Nothing distinguishes a modal Perfekt from a modal Plusquamperfekt except the reference point, so the two forms sit side by side: Er hat nicht kommen können (he was not able to come – relevant now), Er hatte nicht kommen können (he had not been able to come before the next event).

4. Two past events in one sentence

When a sentence carries two past events on different time planes, the earlier one goes into the Plusquamperfekt and the later one into the Präteritum. The order of the events and the order of the clauses are two separate things: in a subordinate clause introduced by nachdem the earlier event comes first, and the finite verb of that clause is last.

Earlier event (Plusquamperfekt)Later event (Präteritum)
Nachdem er den Schlüssel verloren hatte,rief er seinen Vermieter an.
Weil wir den Zug verpasst hatten,mussten wir ein Taxi nehmen.
Sie stieg in die Bahn, nachdem sie am Bahnsteig gewartet hatte. 
Er konnte die Tür nicht öffnen, weil er den Schlüssel eingeschlossen hatte. 
Das Feuer war gelöscht, bevor die Feuerwehr eintraf. 

One very common exception. After als with a Präteritum main clause, the earlier event is often rendered with war plus participle II: Als ich ankam, war er schon gegangen. This is fully idiomatic and needs no explanation.

Two finite Präteritum verbs do occur in one clause, but only when the two actions belong to the same time plane and stand side by side: Wir besuchten die Altstadt, aßen in einem kleinen Restaurant und gingen am Fluss entlang. The moment the two events are not simultaneous in that way, the earlier one becomes a Plusquamperfekt.

5. Präteritum or Perfekt in writing?

The division is not absolute, but it is stable enough to plan with. The Präteritum dominates whenever the text looks backwards; the Perfekt dominates whenever it looks at the past from a point that still matters.

Choose PräteritumChoose Perfekt
Reports, minutes, official statementsEveryday spoken narrative, informal reports to a colleague
Biographical and historical accountsStatements about experience up to today
Literature, film plots, summariesOpening a piece of news and stating the current result
Long stretches of past action with no present relevanceCompleted states: Die Ausstellung ist zu Ende.

A workable split for a written text: use the Präteritum for the process, and the Perfekt for the state that still holds. Der Verein wurde 1998 gegründet und hat seit 2004 dreimal den Titel gewonnen. In a formal letter, the first paragraph is usually better in the Präteritum; a closing sentence about today falls naturally into the Perfekt.

6. Errors that recur

FaultyCorrectReason
Ich hatte in Japan gewesen.Ich war in Japan gewesen.Movement and state of experience take war.
Er hatte nicht kommen können können.Er hatte nicht kommen können.The modal loses its infinitive entirely.
