The sentence bracket and field positionsDie Satzklammer, Position I–III, Nachfeld

1 · The concept

Every German main clause is built as a bracket: the finite verb opens it, and everything else the clause owns closes it. The bracket has three numbered fields, and German lets you put a great deal of material almost anywhere in it — except in three places that never move. What follows is not about the order you must keep, but about the order you are allowed to break and what the sentence looks like afterwards.

The rule in one line. Position I holds the finite verb unless some other element stands in front of it; everything else the clause contains sits in position II; the separable part of a prefixed verb goes to position III; and a fourth slot, the Nachfeld, is the one place where a whole extra clause can hide.

The three fields of the main clause

FieldWhat belongs thereExample
I — Vorfeld / finite verbeither one fronted element, or the conjugated verb in second position Am Montag fahren wir nach Berlin.  ·  Wir fahren am Montag nach Berlin.
II — Mittelfeldthe rest of the clause: subject, objects, complements, adverbs, negation Am Montag fahren wir mit dem Zug nach Berlin.  ·  Wir fahren mit dem Zug nicht nach Berlin.
III — Endfeldthe separable prefix, or the participle of a Perfekt verb Am Montag rufen wir dich an.  ·  Wir haben dich angerufen.
Nachfelda whole extra clause after the closing bracket, introduced by a comma Wir fahren morgen, wenn das Wetter gut ist.

Read the fields as an instruction rather than a list. The finite verb is in position II only when position I is already filled by something else; the non-finite verb part is always last inside its own bracket; and the Nachfeld is optional, never obligatory, and never changes the word order of the main clause it belongs to.

Position I: the field the finite verb vacates

Position I has exactly one inhabitant. If it is not the finite verb, then the finite verb stands in position II and the subject is pushed one step to the right. The verb never stands in position I and keeps its subject in front of it.

What stands in position IEffect on what followsExample
the subject (V2, no inversion)nothing moves; the verb is simply in position II Der Zug kommt um 14 Uhr.
an adverb, a time or a place phrasethe subject follows the verb Um 14 Uhr kommt der Zug.
a negated element (nicht, kein)the subject follows the verb Nicht der Zug ist verspätet.
an object or a complementthe subject follows the verb Diese Entscheidung kann ich nicht akzeptieren.
a whole subordinate clausethe subject follows the verb; the clause keeps its own internal order Dass er kommt, weiß ich nicht.
a clause with a conditional finite verb (würde, sollte)the finite verb comes first, the subject after it Wenn es geht, würde ich kommen.
nothing (yes–no question, imperative)the finite verb itself moves to position I Kommst du mit?  ·  Nimm bitte den zweiten Bus.
The one case that looks like a mistake but is not. When you front a phrase that belongs to the subject, the verb stays in position II and the subject follows normally: Trotz aller Warnungen fuhren wir. Compare Trotz aller Warnungen fuhren wir nicht. — the same fronted phrase, but now it is an adverbial, not a subject attribute, so the verb is fronted and the subject follows it.

Fronting out of position II: what happens to the rest

German is not a fixed-order language. You may pull almost any element out of the Mittelfeld and put it in front. One element moves into position I, the finite verb jumps to position II, and the remaining elements of the Mittelfeld keep their relative order.

Base orderFronted orderWhat changed
Der Professor erklärt den Studenten den Stoff. Den Studenten erklärt der Professor den Stoff. object to position I; verb now in position II; subject after the verb
Wir haben die Übung zwei Mal wiederholt. Zwei Mal haben wir die Übung wiederholt. adverbial to position I; verb in position II; separable part still last
Ich rufe dich heute an. Heute rufe ich dich an. time phrase to position I; verb in position II; an stays in position III
Er ist seit drei Jahren in dieser Firma. Seit drei Jahren ist er in dieser Firma. no inversion at all — the fronted phrase is a subject attribute
Position III does not care. Fronting never touches the closing bracket. In Heute rufe ich dich an the prefix an is still the last thing in the sentence; in Heute habe ich dich angerufen the participle is still last. A separable verb part is the easiest thing in the field system to get right, precisely because it never moves.

Position III: separable and non-separable verbs

TypeStructureExample
separable (trennbar)stem + prefix in position III; the prefix is unstressed Ich rufe dich an.  ·  Der Bus hält in Mainz an.
separable in the Perfektge- goes between the two parts Ich habe dich angerufen.
separable with sein in the Präteritumthe two parts fuse into one word Der Bus kam in Mainz an.  ·  Sie ist in Berlin angekommen.
non-separable (untrennbar)the prefix is unstressed and stays on the verb Ich besuche dich.  ·  Er übersetzt den Text.
ver-, be-, ent-, zer-, emp-, miss-, er-, ge-always untrennbar; the participle takes no ge- Er verspricht es.  ·  Sie hat es versprochen.
modal verbsnever separable; the second verb is a bare infinitive at the end Ich muss dich anrufen.

The three elements you cannot manipulate

German word order looks free because almost everything in it is free. Three things are not, and they are non-negotiable in every register from a telegram to a dissertation.

ElementWhy it is fixedLegal and illegal
the finite verbit opens or anchors the bracket; in position II it is the single fixed point of the clause ✓ Um 14 Uhr kommt der Zug.   ✗ Um 14 Uhr der Zug kommt.
the subject of the main clausefronting the subject keeps V2; if any other element is fronted, the subject follows the verb ✓ Der Zug kommt.   ✗ Der Zug kommt der Zug pünktlich.
the separable part or the participleit closes the bracket and is always last in its own clause ✓ Ich rufe dich an.   ✗ Ich rufe dich an am Abend.
The formal statement. In the main clause the finite verb occupies the second position counting from the left. If the first position is already filled by a fronted element, the finite verb still stands in the second position — the fronted element is counted as a member of the bracket, not as a member of the field. In the subordinate clause there is no front field at all: the finite verb is last, and the position the fronted element would have taken is occupied by the conjunction.

The Nachfeld: the fourth field

The Nachfeld is the stretch after the closing bracket of the main clause, introduced by a comma. It can hold a whole subordinate clause, a relative clause, a prepositional phrase or an infinitive group, and it is closed by the final punctuation mark.

What is extraposedExampleNote
an adverbial clause with a conjunction Wir fahren morgen, wenn das Wetter gut ist. the conjunction takes position I inside the Nachfeld clause; its own verb stays last
a relative clause Das ist das Haus, in dem ich aufgewachsen bin. the relative clause keeps verb-final order inside the Nachfeld
a prepositional phrase Wir warten auf dich, der hinter dem Bahnhof steht. the final adverbial shifts back behind the extraposed phrase without any change of order
an indirect question (ob, wann, warum) Ich weiß nicht, ob er kommt. the question word is optional and takes the position ob would have taken
a second clause joined by und / oder / denn Ich rufe dich an, und dann erzähle ich dir alles. this one has normal main-clause word order, not verb-final order
Nachfeld is not the same as Ausklammerung. Both push material to the end, but they are two different constructions. Er hat, soweit ich weiß, alles vorbereitet breaks the Mittelfeld open and is marked — avoid it. Er hat alles vorbereitet, soweit ich weiß uses the Nachfeld and is neutral to formal. If in doubt, use the Nachfeld.

True Nachfeld: extraposition with es … dass

In extraposition a short es stands in position I or II and the real content follows in the Nachfeld. The word order inside the Nachfeld clause is untouched: ob, dass and wenn occupy its position I, and the verb stays last.

FormExampleComma?
correlate in position II, clause refers to the whole sentence Es ist nicht leicht, dass wir uns treffen. comma — the extraposed clause comments on the whole proposition
correlate in position I Es ist nicht leicht, dass wir uns treffen. same, because the clause still refers back to the whole proposition
correlate in position II, clause belongs to one word Es ist schade, daß er nicht gekommen ist. no comma — the clause belongs to the single correlate schade
dass without a correlate Er hat oft gesagt, daß er kommt. comma — the clause belongs to the whole preceding clause
question word as the extraposed correlative Es ist ungeklärt, warum er gegangen ist. warum takes the position dass would have taken
relative pronoun instead of es Es ist etwas, was mich beschäftigt. no comma when the whole clause is the correlate; with a single-word correlate: Es ist etwas, das mich beschäftigt — comma
The Nachfeld clause has its own fields. You cannot treat the extraposed content as a free block. Es ist nicht leicht, wir treffen uns. is wrong: a dass-clause in the Nachfeld stays verb-final (dass wir uns treffen), and a second main clause joined with und keeps its verb in position II (und dann treffen wir uns).

The Nachfeld with a reflexive-accusative verb

Verbs with a sich-accusative plus a complement form a tight bracket. The content of the complement can be extraposed, but the linking preposition stays in the Mittelfeld and still points forward — and the extraposed clause cannot then be pulled up to position I.

PatternExampleWhat stays where
sich + Akkusativ + preposition Ich freue mich darauf, daß du mitkommst. the preposition remains in the Mittelfeld and governs the extraposed clause
sich + Akkusativ + dass-clause, no preposition Ich freue mich, daß du mitkommst. no preposition may be added; the extraposition is complete
sich + Akkusativ + infinitive group Ich freue mich, dich zu sehen. the zu-infinitive is the extraposed content
sich + Akkusativ, clause fronted as a whole Daß du mitkommst, freue ich mich. fronting a dass-clause is possible; inversion is then obligatory
sich + Akkusativ with subject control Es freut mich, daß du mitkommst. the subject of the main clause stays in the main clause; it is never pulled into the dass-clause
Fronting the dass-clause of a reflexive verb is not free. Ich freue mich, dass du mitkommst can become Daß du mitkommst, freue ich mich, but Dass du mitkommst, ich freue mich is not possible: once the clause stands in position I, the finite verb must occupy position II and the subject must follow it.

The Nachfeld with an infinitive clause

An extraposed content can be an infinitive group rather than a full clause. There is no finite verb, so there is no position I inside it — but the group still counts as a field of the Nachfeld and stays last, after the closing bracket.

Type of infinitive groupExampleNote
zu-infinitive as a complement Ich hoffe, dich bald zu sehen. the preposition that would introduce it stays in the Mittelfeld: Ich hoffe darauf, dich bald zu sehen
um … zu (purpose) Ich stehe früh auf, um den Zug zu erreichen. um stands in the first position of the group, the verb of purpose is last
ohne … zu Er ging weg, ohne sich zu verabschieden. the reflexive pronoun belongs to the extraposed group, not to the main clause
(an)statt … zu Er wartete, statt nach Hause zu gehen. the same structure with a contrastive meaning
bare infinitive after a perception verb Ich sehe dich kommen. no zu; the group simply closes the clause

Stance phrases in position I

Expressions of opinion can occupy the front field on their own. Because they are not clauses, the finite verb moves to position II in exactly the way it does after any other fronted element.

PhraseExampleStructure
der Meinung nach / meiner Meinung nach Der Meinung nach ist er im Vorteil. genitive phrase: definite article for die Meinung, possessive for meine Meinung
meiner Ansicht nach Meiner Ansicht nach war das ein Fehler. no article; identical position I behaviour
meines Erachtens Meines Erachtens war das ein Fehler. fixed phrase, genitive form, no article, never inflected
meinerseits / deinerseits Meinerseits wäre ich dafür. adverbial form; Konjunktiv II remains possible in position II
in meinen Augen / meines Erachtens nach … zu urteilen In meinen Augen ist das keine Lösung. an ordinary prepositional phrase — it may equally stay in the Mittelfeld
All of them put the verb in position II. Meines Erachtens war das ein Fehler, never Meines Erachtens das war ein Fehler. And they are not simply interchangeable: der Meinung nach can also report somebody else's opinion when the possessor is named — Der Meinung nach seiner Mutter war das richtig. meines Erachtens can only ever be the speaker's own view.

Comparative pairs in position I: je … desto

Je … desto … is a correlative pair whose first half fills position I with a clause-like unit. The first half keeps verb-final order; the second half is a full main clause with the verb in position II. In writing, umso is the more precise and more usual alternative.

FormExampleWord order
je …, desto … (spoken) Je früher du kommst, desto länger können wir reden. first half verb-final, second half with the verb in position II
je …, umso … (written) Je mehr man übt, umso sicherer wird man. identical structure; umso is neutral in register
first half without a correlative Je mehr man übt, desto sicherer wird man. if desto / umso is missing, the second half may take any main-clause order
desto after a negative first half Je öfter man wartet, desto ärger wird er. the correlative is obligatory for the marked, inverted reading
reversed order ✓ Je mehr man übt, desto besser wird man. ✗ Desto besser, je mehr man übt — the two halves may not be swapped
Why the second half inverts. Desto besser is the field in position I of the second clause, so the finite verb wird takes position II and the subject man follows it. This is not a stylistic inversion; it is the normal V2 order for a clause with a fronted field.

Emphasis on the Nachfeld: Was mich betrifft, …

To put a whole clause into position I and shift the emphasis onto it, the clause must be fronted as a unit. The subject then follows the finite verb, and the Nachfeld stays empty.

Fronted unitExampleEffect
relative clause with was Was mich betrifft, habe ich keine Einwände. the fronted clause is position I; the main clause inverts; the Nachfeld is unused
was + subject + participle Was die Kosten betrifft, können wir das besprechen. the Perfekt participle stays last: it still closes the second bracket
fronted dass-clause Dass er nicht kommt, müssen wir das entscheiden. verb-final order inside the fronted clause, V2 in the main clause
fronted indirect question Wann er kommt, ist noch offen. the question word stands in position I inside the fronted group
fronted without inversion Was mich betrifft, das stimmt. possible only because das is the subject; a dass-clause here would be wrong
Fronting a Nachfeld clause is not the same as writing it in the Nachfeld. Was mich betrifft, habe ich keine Einwände (fronted, inverted) and Ich habe keine Einwände, was mich betrifft (Nachfeld) are both possible and mean almost the same — but a bare clause with its own subject may not be put in position I: Was das betrifft, ich habe keine Einwände is ungrammatical, because the subject of a fronted clause cannot also serve as the subject of the following main clause.

Do not confuse these

PairDifference
position I vs. position IIposition I holds either one fronted element or the finite verb, never both. Position II holds the finite verb only when position I is already filled.
position II vs. position IIIposition II is the whole open Mittelfeld; position III is the single closing slot for the separable part or the participle. In Ich rufe dich an, an is position III and dich is position II.
Nachfeld vs. Ausklammerungthe Nachfeld is a field of its own with its own word order, introduced by a comma and closed by the full stop. Ausklammerung pulls an element out of the Mittelfeld across the closing bracket; it is marked and normally wrong in formal German.
Nachfeld clause vs. second main clausea subordinate clause in the Nachfeld is verb-final; a second main clause joined by und / oder / denn / aber keeps its verb in position II: …, weil er kommt vs. …, und dann kommt er.
fronted subordinate clause vs. extrapositionfronting puts the clause in position I and forces inversion in the main clause; extraposition leaves position I to a short es and leaves the main clause uninverted: Dass er kommt, weiß ich vs. Es ist klar, dass er kommt.
der Meinung nach vs. meiner Meinung nach vs. meines Erachtensidentical in position and in effect on word order. der for a general or third-party opinion, meiner for the speaker's own, meines Erachtens a fixed formal phrase. All three put the finite verb in position II.
fronting a subject attribute vs. fronting an adverbiala fronted phrase that belongs to the subject does not cause inversion (Trotz allem sind wir gefahren); a fronted adverbial does (Trotz allem fahren wir nicht).
